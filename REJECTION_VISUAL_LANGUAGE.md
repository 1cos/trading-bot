# Rejection Visual Language

> **Purpose:** Describe how Max visually recognizes a beautiful rejection
> candle. Not how to detect it — what his eye sees.
> **Not included:** Thresholds, formulas, rules, or implementation.

---

## What Max Notices

### Someone clearly won

The candle tells a story with one winner. For a rejection at support,
buyers won. For a rejection at resistance, sellers won. You can tell
who won by looking at where the candle closed relative to where it
opened and where it ventured. The losing side made an attempt — the
wick proves they tried — but by the close, the winning side had taken
control completely.

### The wick tells the story of a failed attempt

The wick reaching into the level is the most important part of the
candle. It says: price went there, tested that area, and was pushed
back. The longer the wick relative to the body, the more violent the
rejection was. The wick is the scar of a battle that one side lost
decisively. It reached into enemy territory and was thrown out.

### The close inspires confidence

The close is far from the level. Not just technically on the right
side — comfortably on the right side. Looking at where the candle
closed, you feel that the level held with room to spare. The close
does not make you nervous. It does not make you wonder if the level
will break on the next candle. It makes you feel that the argument
is settled.

### The body is substantial

The body of the rejection candle is large enough to represent real
commitment. The market did not just drift to a close — it was pushed
there. The body shows that after the wick tested the level, the
market moved with conviction in the opposite direction. A substantial
body means participants acted, not just waited.

### The candle looks decisive on its own

If you isolate the rejection candle from everything around it, it
still looks like something happened. It does not look like a random
bar in the middle of noise. It looks like a candle where a decision
was made. You could show it to someone with no context and they would
say "something happened here."

### The candle finishes its work

A beautiful rejection candle does not leave unfinished business. It
does not close in the middle of its range, suggesting more fighting
to come. It closes near one extreme of its range, as if the matter
is resolved. The candle opened, explored, decided, and committed.
The work is done within this single bar.

### The rejection feels instantaneous

The best rejection candles give the impression that the level snapped
price back. There is no gradual negotiation. Price reached the zone
and was immediately repelled, like a hand touching something hot. The
wick is sharp, not rounded. The reversal within the candle was fast,
not slow.

### The candle looks different from its neighbors

A rejection candle stands out. The surrounding candles may be smaller,
quieter, less directional. The rejection candle is visually distinct
— bigger body, longer wick into the level, more range. It announces
itself. If all the candles around it look the same, it is not a
rejection — it is just another bar.

## What Max Does NOT Notice

### Both sides fought equally

If the candle has long wicks on both ends and a small body in the
middle, nobody won. The market went up, went down, and ended up
nowhere. This is indecision, not rejection. Both buyers and sellers
had their say, and neither prevailed. The candle does not resolve
anything — it creates more uncertainty.

### The body is tiny

A candle that touches the level but closes with a barely visible body
is not convincing. It whispers instead of shouting. The wick may be
there, but the body says the market did not actually commit to leaving
the level. A doji at a level is interesting as information, but it is
not a rejection that Max would trade.

### The candle finishes in the middle of its range

If the close sits near the center of the candle's range — equidistant
from the high and the low — the candle has not decided anything. It
explored both directions and landed in no man's land. A rejection
candle must finish its work near one end, not in the middle.

### The close is barely past the level

A candle that touches the level and closes just a hair on the right
side is technically a rejection but visually unconvincing. The close
is so close to the level that the next candle could easily push back
through. There is no margin of safety. The level may have held, but
just barely, and "just barely" does not inspire confidence.

### The candle looks like everything around it

If the rejection candle has the same size, same range, and same
character as the candles before and after it, nothing special
happened. The rejection candle must be visually distinct from the
surrounding noise. If it blends in, it is not a rejection — it is
just another candle that happened to touch the level.

### The wick is on the wrong side

A candle at a support level that has a long wick above the body and
a tiny wick below is not rejecting the support — it is rejecting
higher prices. The wick must reach into the level to show that the
level was tested. A wick pointing away from the level tells a
different story: the market tried to leave the level and was pulled
back toward it. That is not rejection. That is failure to escape.

### The candle is noisy and chaotic

Some candles have wide ranges, long wicks on both sides, and bodies
that seem randomly placed. They cover a lot of ground but tell no
clear story. These candles happened during confusion, not during a
decision. A rejection is a decision. If the candle looks confused,
the market was confused, and confused is not rejected.

### There is no wick into the level at all

If the candle closes on the right side of the level but never actually
reached into it — the low (for support) never touched the zone — then
the level was not tested. You cannot reject something you never
touched. The candle may be bullish, it may be strong, but it is not
a rejection candle at this level. It is just a candle that happened
nearby.
